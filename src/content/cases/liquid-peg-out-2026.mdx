On **6 September 2026** the **Liquid Network**, a Bitcoin sidechain with a
federated peg, paid about **3,996 BTC** from its mainchain peg wallet. Liquid and SideSwap both say **no authorization key was stolen**. The
request went through SideSwap’s Peg-out Authorization Key (PAK) and was signed
as a normal peg-out. Other issued assets on Liquid were not reported minted;
the **L-BTC backing was**.

No public criminal charging document is cited here. Legal status:
**unresolved**. Case status: **open**.

## What the public record establishes

### The payout (Bitcoin)

According to Blockstream Explorer, Bitcoin transaction
[`8db751a650ae2f12006b7e8c69a75e4df360e8afd6b9e05ae0b9fa6458a7b140`](https://blockstream.info/tx/8db751a650ae2f12006b7e8c69a75e4df360e8afd6b9e05ae0b9fa6458a7b140)
confirmed in **block 965,783** at **14:28:56 UTC** on 6 Sep 2026 and paid
**3,996.01834922 BTC** to `bc1qgslsydz56d0ed6827hdemfmk5w2f6ldyc6wt7p`.

SideSwap stated that at **14:28 UTC** the federation paid **3,996 BTC** to
the customer’s Bitcoin address after a customer sent **4,000 L-BTC** to
SideSwap’s peg-out service at **14:05 UTC**.

Liquid’s incident post (20:25 UTC) described about **4,000 BTC**
(**$320 million**) withdrawn by “purported white-hat hackers,” and said
Blockstream was contacting them on-chain with a signed message.

Hours after the peg-out, Bitcoin transaction
[`c103de95817b43f2df635ec6f35ff126ca26a7c6d20570c4b01866b2b3e69a19`](https://blockstream.info/tx/c103de95817b43f2df635ec6f35ff126ca26a7c6d20570c4b01866b2b3e69a19)
carries an OP_RETURN reading **“we are whitehats. contact us on chain.”**
That is the actors’ self-description. A return is confirmed only when
bitcoin moves to an address the federation controls.

Bitquery’s reconstruction (verified 7 Sep 2026) puts about **197.47 BTC**
left in the federation peg wallet after the payout, down from about
**4,200 BTC** before it, and treats later OP_RETURN traffic between the
parties as an open thread. Those reserve figures are Bitquery’s snapshot,
not a Liquid official remaining-balance statement.

### Keys, PAK, and other assets

Liquid stated the funds were withdrawn via **SideSwap’s PAK**, and that **that
key was not compromised, nor were any others**. SideSwap stated the same, and
that no SideSwap system was compromised: the desk “had no way to tell those
coins from any other L-BTC.”

Liquid also stated that **other Liquid assets such as USDT, DePix, and RWAs
are unaffected**. The L-BTC peg — bitcoin locked by the federation to back
Liquid Bitcoin one-for-one — is the reserve that moved.

Liquid documentation describes PAK as a whitelist so that even compromised
functionaries cannot redirect peg-outs to attacker-controlled addresses. A
valid PAK signature on a burn of L-BTC that the network treated as real is
exactly the path this incident used. PAK does not, by itself, prove the burned
L-BTC was ever backed.

### Pause

Liquid stated that exchanges had been notified to pause L-BTC deposits and
withdrawals, and that **bridge nodes were temporarily disabled**, so “no new
transactions can be submitted to the network.” SideSwap paused swaps, peg-ins,
and peg-outs until the network resumes.

### Bitcoin itself

Nothing in the vendor statements or the Bitcoin transaction alleges a failure
of Bitcoin’s proof-of-work consensus. The coins left a **federation-controlled
multisig** on Bitcoin, following Liquid’s peg-out rules as the functionaries
applied them.

## Technical analysis

Synthesized from the public Elements git history and independent
reconstructions. **Blockstream has not published a postmortem** naming the bug
as of last review. What follows is attributed reconstruction, not a vendor
finding.

### Confidential amounts and the range-proof cache

Liquid uses Elements confidential transactions: amounts (and often assets) are
hidden behind Pedersen commitments. Nodes cannot see the numbers, so they check
a **range proof** that a hidden amount is non-negative. Without that check, a
hidden **negative** output can balance a large positive output and print
money.

Range proofs are expensive relative to other checks. Elements therefore
memoizes a pass in `CachingRangeProofChecker` (`src/script/sigcache.cpp`). If
the cache returns a hit, `secp256k1_rangeproof_verify` is not run again for
that entry, for the lifetime of the process.

### Two cache-key defects, same function

Independent write-ups point at that cache. They do not all describe the same
defect.

**2019 omission (context not in the key).** For years the cache key hashed
the **proof bytes and the value commitment only**. `secp256k1_rangeproof_verify`
also authenticates the **asset generator** and the output **scriptPubKey**
(`extra_commit`). A proof verified in one (asset, script) context could later
return cached-true in another. Calle, orangesurf, and Bitquery describe this
shape: plant a valid proof, then reuse its cache entry on an invalid statement.
Orangesurf dates the caching optimisation to about seven years before the
incident; mononaut dates the key “simplification” that dropped asset and
script to **19 March 2019**.

**2026 unframed concatenation (the public patch).** Commit
[`c26d719`](https://github.com/ElementsProject/elements/commit/c26d719c29a40da280a825b25657e9c3d8bc7d99)
(author date **3 Aug 2026**) changes `ComputeEntryRangeProof` to also write
the asset commitment and scriptPubKey into the SHA-256 hasher. The four
fields are concatenated **without length prefixes or other framing**. Proof
and scriptPubKey are variable-length. Independent lab work on the on-chain
pair — primers in Liquid block **4,050,335**, invalid output in
[`f24a4b17…0a183f`](https://blockstream.info/liquid/tx/f24a4b179b5cc7e88b25a763911f7cbdf2bf45d1d1b5ab611e94461cef0a183f)
in **4,050,336** — shows **identical cache preimages under that unframed
post-fix key**. Mononaut’s reconstruction: a primer OP_RETURN with a valid
proof, then an invalid proof padded so the concatenated bytes collide; the
invalid output is unspendable (OP_RETURN), and a second confidential output
carries a large in-range positive. That collision **requires the public
patch**, not only the 2019 omission.

The 2019 omission is real code history. It is **not**, on the public record,
proven as the path that production acceptors ran on 6 September.

### What the on-chain pair shows, and what it does not

Bitquery traces a day of small Liquid transactions that repeatedly planted the
same hidden-amount OP_RETURN and range proof, then a mint at **13:53 UTC** in
block **4,050,336**, SideSwap sweeps, and the Bitcoin payout at **14:28 UTC**.
Bitquery is explicit that amounts on Liquid are hidden, that Blockstream has
not named the bug, and that the link from planted outputs to the cache fix is
a **fit, not a confirmation**.

Mononaut argues that nodes still on official releases (without `c26d719`)
**rejected** the exploit block and stalled at height **4,050,335**, and that
the padding in `f24a4b17…` only produces a cacheable key under the new
format. That is evidence that **some** acceptors ran a post-`c26d719` binary.
It is **not** a public inventory of which functionaries or watchmen ran
which binary.

Whether production acceptors actually ran that patched (and still unframed)
binary remains **unproven** in the sources cited. Liquid and SideSwap say
“an Elements bug”; they do not say which.

## Why it matters for Bitcoin history

1. **A federated peg is not Bitcoin consensus.** The coins moved because
   functionaries signed a Bitcoin transaction that the peg rules treated as
   authorized. Bitcoin’s proof-of-work was not the failure.
2. **PAK is not a backing check.** A valid peg-out authorization proves the
   burn was requested by a whitelisted desk. It does not prove the burned
   L-BTC was issued against locked BTC.
3. **Confidential-transaction caches are consensus.** A performance memoization
   that omits verification context, or that frames variable-length fields
   ambiguously, can split a network: primed nodes accept, unprimed nodes
   reject, and the block bytes look the same.
4. **A public patch is not a deployment.** The cache bind was on `master` five
   days before the payout and on the 23.3.x line hours **after** it. Open
   source made the change inspectable; it did not by itself put a tagged
   binary on the boxes that signed.

## Reading notes

- This is **a peg-out enabled by a software defect**, not a court case with a
  charged or convicted defendant in the sources above.
- Do not equate “Elements bug” with “SideSwap stole funds” or “Blockstream
  stole funds.” Both operators deny a stolen key.
- “Whitehat” is the actors’ on-chain self-description (OP_RETURN in
  `c103de95…e69a19`) and Liquid’s “purported” label. A return is confirmed
  only when bitcoin moves to an address the federation controls; that had
  not been shown as a completed fact at last review.
- Bitquery’s remaining-reserve snapshot is dated **7 Sep 2026** and will
  move if coins return or more payouts occur. Do not treat it as a frozen
  backing ratio.
- Prefer Liquid and SideSwap statements plus Bitcoin/Liquid explorers for
  **what moved**. Prefer the Elements git history for **what the patch
  changed**. Prefer independent reconstructions (Bitquery, mononaut, calle,
  orangesurf) only as **attributed** mechanism hypotheses.
- Do not treat the 2019 omission as the proven production exploit. Lab work
  on the 4,050,335 / 4,050,336 pair is consistent with the **unframed 2026
  key**.
- Do not treat the 17:21 UTC merge of PR #1599 as proof that production was
  unpatched, or as proof that it was patched. It is a git timestamp after
  the payout.
- Bitcoin base-layer consensus was not affected. Other issued Liquid assets
  were reported unaffected; L-BTC backing was not.
- No Blockstream postmortem is cited. When one appears, this file should be
  reopened and `last_reviewed` updated.
